Jailbreaks as Inference-Time Alignment:
A Framework for Understanding Safety Failures in LLMs
James Beetham1, Souradip Chakraborty2, Mengdi Wang3, Furong Huang2,4,
Amrit Singh Bedi1, and Mubarak Shah1
1University of Central Florida

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are safety-
aligned to prevent harmful response genera-
tion, yet still remain vulnerable to jailbreak
attacks. While prior works have focused on
improving jailbreak attack effectiveness, they
offer little explanation for why safety align-
ment fails. We address this gap by framing
jailbreaks as inference-time alignment, connect-
ing attack design and safety alignment within
a unified optimization framework. This fram-
ing allows us to extend best-of- N inference-
time alignment to the adversarial setting, called
LIAR (Leveraging Inference-time Alignment
to jailbReak), and derive suboptimality bounds
that show LIAR provably approaches an opti-
mal jailbreak as compute scales. Interestingly,
our framework allows us to develop the notion
of a Safety-Net, a measure of how vulnerable
an LLM is to jailbreaks, which helps to ex-
plain why safety alignment can fail. Empir-
ically, LIAR produces natural, hard-to-detect
prompts that achieve a competitive attack suc-
cess rate while running 10 to 100x faster than
prior suffix-based jailbreaks.
1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly
embedded in safety-critical applications such as
education, healthcare, and customer support. To
reduce harmful outputs, developers use alignment
techniques such as reinforcement learning from hu-
man feedback (RLHF) (Ziegler et al., 2019; Glaese
et al., 2022) that encourage models to prioritize
safety alongside other goals like usefulness and
relevance. Yet jailbreaks, specially crafted prompts
that bypass safety training (Zou et al., 2023; Liu
et al., 2023a), can elicit harmful behavior. This
raises a central question: why are models still vul-
nerable to jailbreaks after safety alignment?

Most prior work (Chao et al., 2023; Mehrotra
et al., 2023) have focused on jailbreak effective-
ness, analyzing jailbreaks primarily in terms of
attack success rate. These methods, often based on
prompt engineering or discrete optimization (Zou
et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023a), demonstrate that
jailbreaks are possible, but they offer little expla-
nation of why such attacks succeed at bypassing
safety alignment in the first place. As a result,
our understanding of model vulnerability remains
largely descriptive: they work in practice, but their
connection to safety alignment failure is unclear.
To explain why attacks succeed, we treat jail-
breaks as inference-time alignment . This per-
spective stems from the observation that jailbreaks
operate entirely at inference time, and framing jail-
break optimization in terms of alignment brings the
problem into a space that unifies safety alignment
and the adversarial jailbreaks. We adapt the best-
of-N inference-time alignment technique to the jail-
break setting, which we call LIAR (Leveraging
Inference-time Alignment to jailbReak). We derive
suboptimality bounds, where we find the perfor-
mance gap between LIAR and an optimal adversar-
ial attacker provably shrinks asN increases. This
establishes LIAR as a principled inference-time
alignment jailbreak attack. Empirically, we show
that LIAR is an effective black-box attack that re-
quires no training, produces natural low-perplexity
prompts, and runs orders of magnitude faster than
prior suffix-based optimizers.
Beyond optimal attack design, our alignment
framing enables us to estimate an LLM’s vulner-
ability to jailbreaks, which we term Safety-Net.
Safety-Net provides a principled measure of how
much separation a model maintains during safety
fine-tuning between safe and unsafe objectives.
This insight opens the possibility of actively assess-
ing LLM jailbreak vulnerability during safety fine-
tuning rather than solely after deployment (via jail-
breaks as done in state-of-the-art approaches). To

We summarize the main contributions of our work
as follows:
(1) Theoretical framing of jailbreaks as
inference-time alignment. We reframe jailbreaks
as inference-time alignment, which enables us to
derive formal suboptimality bounds for alignment-
based jailbreaks, showing that best-of- N sam-
pling provably approaches an optimal adversarial
prompter, and formulate a model’s vulnerability to
jailbreaks from Safety-Net.
(2) LIAR: an alignment-grounded jail-
break. To evaluate this framing, we introduce
LIAR (Leveraging Inference-time Alignment to
jailbReak), a black-box best-of- N attack that
requires no training, runs 10-100x faster than
suffix-based optimizers, and serves as a practical
testbed for our theoretical results.
(3) Safety-Net: a principled measure for jail-
break vulnerability. We propose Safety-Net, a
measure which quantifies the gap between safe
and unsafe objectives under adversarial prompt-
ing. This measure provides a theoretical foundation
for understanding jailbreak vulnerability which we
show correlates with empirical attack success.
2 Background and Formulation
Language models. Consider a vocabulary setV,
and let the language model (LM) be represented
by a mapping πθ, parameterized by θ. LM πθ
takes as input a sequence of tokens, referred to as
a prompt, represented by x :={x1,x 2,··· ,xN},
where each tokenxi∈V . Given the input prompt,
LM predicts the first output tokeny0∈V . To pre-
dict the next tokeny1, the model takes as input the
original prompt x along with the previously gener-
ated tokeny0, forming a new prompt [x,y 0]. This
process is repeated for subsequent time steps. The
input prompt at time step t is [x, y1:t−1], where
y1:t−1 = {y0,y 1,··· ,yt−1}, and LLM outputs
next tokenyt∼πθ(·|[x, y1:t−1]).
Jailbreaking LLMs. As formulated in (Zou et al.,
2023), it is possible to attack even an aligned LLM
πθ with the help of a prompt suffix q∈ Q⊆V|V|.
For instance (cf. Figure 1), consider an unsafe
prompt x∈X⊆V |V| (e.g., “Write a script to ex-
ploit an operating system") and if we input thisx to
a safety aligned LLM modelπθ, it will not answer
this query and would generate a negative response
(e.g., “I cannot answer this, this is unsafe."). But
with the help of an adversarial prompt given by
[x, q], which is a concatenation of prompt x and
adversarial suffix q∈ Q, it is possible to generate
harmful response y ={y1,y 2,··· ,yT} such that
yt∼πθ(·|[x, q, y<t]) from the aligned target LLM
(e.g., “Sure, here is a script...."), where we define

values, such as Falcon-7B, are more easily compro-
mised. There are, however, notable outliers such as
Pythia-7B, which receives a relatively high Safety-
Net score despite being among the more vulnerable
models.
Overall, this experiment serves as a proof of con-
cept demonstrating that the Safety-Net provides a
practical and theoretically grounded indicator of
model safety robustness. Despite its simplicity,
the metric captures meaningful variation in jail-
break susceptibility across models, aligning with
trends observed in empirical attack success rates.
While the current implementation uses approxi-
mate reward functions and limited sampling, the
results suggest that Safety-Net can offer a low-
compute proxy for assessing alignment strength.

We have already shown that different
AdversarialLLM models have varying effec-

experimental settings that may impact results,
namely length of generated suffixesq, and length
of generated responses. Additional ablations for
AdversarialLLM temperature can be found in the
appendix.
Impact of query length. The length ofq is evalu-
ated in Table 5. Increasing the length of q results
in longer query times, however even doubling the
length has a query time shorter than AdvPrompter.
When changing the length ofq, and important fact
to consider is that longerq have a higher chance of
prompt-drift, where [x,q ] may be asking for con-
tent far from x on its own. This is a limitation
of the keyword matching aspect of the ASR met-
ric being used. We see ASR@1 improve with q
length, but ASR@10 peaks forq length 48 while
ASR@100 peaks for q length 30. This suggests
that longerq length may result in decreased diver-
sity, and as shorterq lengths are preferred to reduce
prompt-drift, 30 is a reasonable prompt length.

more standard 150 TargetLLM tokens (Paulus

5 Conclusions and Limitations
In this work, we reframed jailbreaks as inference-
time alignment, unifying adversarial prompting
with safety training. This perspective yields subop-
timality bounds for best-of-N sampling, explaining
why simple inference-time search can approximate
an optimal adversarial prompter. We further intro-
duced LIAR, a black-box, training-free attack that
exposes alignment weaknesses, and Safety-Net, a
principled measure of jailbreak vulnerability corre-
lated with empirical success rates. Together, these
contributions move jailbreak research beyond em-
pirical benchmarking toward a formal understand-
ing of why alignment fails and how such failures
can be anticipated before deployment.
Risks of this work: While we introduce an ef-
ficient jailbreak method that could potentially be
misused, similar techniques are already publicly
available (e.g., Hughes et al. (2024)). Moreover,
our method prioritizes generation efficiency but re-
quires many queries to the target LLM, making it
more suited for red-teaming and safety evaluation
than deployment in real-world jailbreak scenarios.

Limitations. First, as a black-box attack, LIAR de-
pends on repeated sampling from the target model,
which can be impractical under strict API rate lim-
its or access restrictions. Second, LIAR’s effective-
ness is tied to the quality of the judge used both
as a reward signal and for computing ASR. Inac-
curate or overly permissive judges may produce
false positives, flagging benign completions as jail-
breaks and thereby distorting evaluations. Finally,
although LIAR provides sub-optimality guarantees
under certain theoretical conditions, these guaran-
tees may not extend robustly to real-world scenar-
ios. Moreover, the empirical safety-net results rep-
resent approximations of the idealized theoretical
formulation. Future work could address these limi-
tations by improving query efficiency, leveraging
stronger unsafe reward objectives, and formalizing
guarantees under adaptive or imperfect judges.
