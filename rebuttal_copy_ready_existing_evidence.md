## Response to Reviewer u76e

Thank you for identifying places where the main-paper presentation obscured both the definitions and the scope of our evaluation. We agree that these points should not require readers to reconstruct information from the figures or appendix.

**Terminology and notation.** By “conversation root,” we mean the state before the assistant emits its first token. A reached state additionally contains the assistant-side history, \(s_t=x\oplus h_{\leq t}\). We will introduce this distinction before first using “root” and explicitly define \(r_0/u_0\) as the recovery/harmful alternatives at the root, \(r_t/u_t\) as the corresponding alternatives from \(s_t\), \(\pi_\theta\) as the trained policy, and \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) as the frozen reference. We will likewise define both anchor losses and their weights immediately below Eq. 9.

**Evaluation scope.** Only S1 is a constructed-insertion suite. S2 uses unseen offsets of 128, 256, and 512 tokens, all beyond the 80-token training cap. S3 uses checkpoint-generated, non-spliced states and separately covers self-generated continuations, tool/RAG contamination, and multi-turn drift. CURE-DPO obtains 24.6/14.8/19.2% ASR on these three S3 families, compared with 54.1/45.0/48.6% for Base. S4 then optimizes the attack against each defended checkpoint rather than against Base; at 256 queries, CURE-DPO obtains 33.1% ASR versus 69.0% for Base. We will move the S3 family breakdown into the main text so that this scope is visible without consulting the appendix. We agree that these remain controlled, benchmark-generated trajectories rather than deployment logs, and will state this boundary explicitly.

**Baseline rationale.** Our inclusion criteria were published training-time defenses that explicitly target unsafe-prefix or mid-generation robustness, can be reproduced on open-weight models, and admit evaluation under the same data, optimization-budget, attack, and judge protocol. This gives deep safety alignment, backtracking, circuit breakers, DOOR/W-DOOR, and PRESTO. Inference-time any-depth defenses are compositional with a training objective rather than mutually exclusive alternatives. We will state these criteria before listing the baselines and clarify that the comparison is not intended to cover every safety intervention.

We would also like to draw attention to a result that we currently report only in the appendix, because it speaks to whether the reproductions are fair. Before applying our protocol, we reproduce a headline number from each method in *its own* original setting, and every one of the six sanity checks falls within a single seed SD of the published value. The same checkpoints then behave very differently under our suites: the circuit-breaker reproduction reaches 3.9% attack success in its own prefilling evaluation but 29.3% on S1, deep safety alignment reaches 3.0% versus 34.1%, and DOOR reaches 5.6% versus 25.8%. This gap is not a reproduction failure — it quantifies how much of the published mid-generation robustness is tied to the particular state distribution on which it was measured, typically a single shallow prefix, a single harmful source, and a single judge. We read this as one of the paper's substantive findings rather than a caveat, and we will promote it from the appendix into the main comparison.

**AUROC and the incorrect pointer.** AUROC 0.779 means that, for a randomly drawn successful and unsuccessful held-out attack instance, \(-\)LTRM ranks the successful instance as riskier 77.9% of the time. The corresponding root-margin value is 64.8%, with a prompt-clustered paired difference of \(+0.131\), 95% CI \([0.109,0.154]\). We will add this interpretation to the Figure 3(a) discussion. The reviewer is also correct that the softmax-weighted alternatives are reported in the appendix, not in Section 6.5 as the current sentence states. We will correct the cross-reference and summarize the sweep’s main result in the main paper: every concentrated rule improves over averaging all six states, while top-1, top-3, and low-temperature softmax weighting remain within 0.5 pp of top-2.

We appreciate these comments and will make the definitions, evaluation coverage, baseline scope, and diagnostic interpretation self-contained in the revision.

## Response to Reviewer ptEw

Thank you for identifying two controls that are important for interpreting the attribution. We agree with the distinctions underlying both concerns.

**Continuation-source and surface-form confounding.** Holding the reached state byte-for-byte fixed removes differences in the conditioning context, but it does not by itself eliminate every stylistic or topical difference between the two continuations. The submitted protocol reduces this concern in four ways: (i) an NLI filter requires the harmful branch to preserve the original intent; (ii) branch lengths must have a ratio in \([0.67,1.50]\) and differ by at most 64 tokens; (iii) evaluation uses a held-out harmful-response source, including the fully separated diagnostic protocol; and (iv) two authors independently audited 100 pairs. In this audit, 93/100 recovery branches were safe and helpful, 95/100 harmful branches preserved the intended harmful task, and 89/100 satisfied both criteria, with pair-level \(\kappa=0.79\).

One property of the design is worth making explicit, because it rules out the specific version of this confound that would most favor our method. The pair pool is constructed exactly once and then frozen: every recovery branch is sampled from the *frozen, untrained base* checkpoint, and all subsequent checkpoints are re-scored on that identical text. CURE-DPO therefore cannot earn a higher recovery margin by preferring its own learned refusal phrasing, since the branch it is scored on was written by a model that never received our training signal. For the same reason, the reversal rates in Figure 1b (47.9% at Base down to 16.1% for CURE-DPO) differ only in the scoring model, not in the ranked branches. This is stated in Appendix B; we will move it into Section 3 where the identical-state control is introduced.

These controls reduce but do not fully identify safety independently of continuation style. We will therefore revise “only the continuation differs” to make its precise meaning explicit: the statement describes the token-level identical-state control, not a claim that safety is the only remaining semantic attribute. We will also foreground residual branch-distribution confounding in the limitations and avoid describing the learned signal as “pure” safety recovery.

The effect is nevertheless not confined to the training branch pool. LTRM remains predictive under the fully separated protocol, which changes the branch source, attack family, outcome-state realization, and judge (AUROC 0.779 versus 0.648 for the root margin). CURE-DPO also improves on checkpoint-generated S3 states that are not constructed from the training pair pool: 19.6% ASR versus 49.3% for Base, with 24.6/14.8/19.2% on self-generated, tool/RAG, and multi-turn states. We view these as out-of-distribution behavioral evidence, while retaining the reviewer’s confounding point as a limitation of mechanistic attribution.

**Recovery SFT and lower-tail selection.** Recovery SFT uses the same six reached states per prompt, recovery texts, prompts, anchors, scored-token and optimizer-step budgets, learning rate, model capacity, and three seeds as the same-state DPO cells. It does not receive the final dynamic top-2 aggregation, and we agree that this distinction should be stated more clearly.

The submitted factorial makes two separate matched contrasts rather than attributing their sum to DPO. First, under uniform aggregation, changing only the objective from Recovery SFT to Uniform Same-State DPO lowers S1 ASR from 37.4% to 26.1%, a difference of \(-11.3\) pp with 95% CI \([-13.9,-8.7]\). Second, holding DPO fixed, changing uniform aggregation to the top-2 rule lowers S1 ASR from 26.1% to 20.2%, a difference of \(-5.9\) pp with 95% CI \([-7.9,-3.9]\). Thus the existing evidence identifies an objective effect at uniform aggregation and an aggregation effect under DPO; it does not estimate their interaction. We will state this limitation explicitly and will not imply that the missing lower-tail-SFT cross-cell has already been tested.

**Benign long-form behavior.** We agree that XSTest, OR-Bench-Hard, IFEval, and MMLU-Pro do not measure premature abandonment after a benign long response has begun. The submitted results establish only that CURE-DPO changes root/short-response ORR from 6.5% to 7.6%, IFEval from 89.3 to 88.3, and MMLU-Pro from 77.6 to 77.0. We will rename the relevant discussion so it does not suggest comprehensive over-refusal coverage and will elevate benign long-form abandonment as an unmeasured, method-specific risk. We will not claim that the anchors resolve this behavior.

**Model scope.** Our evidence covers two English, text-only 8–9B instruction models and one full-parameter recipe. On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, transferred without retuning, CURE-DPO obtains 24.7% S1 ASR and 21.1% S3 ASR, versus 64.6% and 54.1% for Base. This supports transfer across the two tested model families but not generalization to 70B+, RLHF-only, multimodal, or reasoning-focused models. We will ensure that all claims remain within this scope.

## Response to Reviewer FaVn

Thank you for emphasizing external validity and coverage of the reachable state space. We agree that a sampled-state metric is not a robustness certificate.

**Coverage of unsafe states.** LTRM is explicitly an offline statistic over sampled states and places no bound on failures outside that distribution. The submitted evaluation progressively moves away from the training construction: S2 uses unseen depths of 128, 256, and 512 tokens; S3 uses checkpoint-generated, non-spliced states; and S4 adapts the attack to each defended checkpoint. At the deepest 512-token states, CURE-DPO obtains 38.7% ASR versus 74.6% for Base. Under the adaptive 256-query attack it obtains 33.1% versus 69.0%. These experiments narrow the sampling concern but do not eliminate it, and we will sharpen this limitation rather than imply full reachable-space coverage.

**Unsafe-state types.** The S3 results already separate the categories requested by the reviewer. CURE-DPO obtains 24.6% ASR on self-generated unsafe states, 14.8% on tool/RAG-contaminated states, and 19.2% on multi-turn drift, compared with 54.1%, 45.0%, and 48.6% for Base. The accepted-state-weighted prompt failure rate is 17.1% for CURE-DPO versus 45.8% for Base. We will move this breakdown from the appendix into the main results and report the state-proposal yield alongside the conditional ASR.

**Model-family scope.** We replicate the central comparison on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct without retuning the method: CURE-DPO obtains 24.7% S1 ASR, 21.1% S3 ASR, and 74.2% recovery, compared with 64.6%, 54.1%, and 17.2% for Base. This is evidence for transfer across two tested model families, not for larger, multimodal, or reasoning-focused systems. We will make that boundary more prominent.

**Long trajectories, benign behavior, and inference-time defenses.** Our current depth evaluation stops at 512 tokens, so it does not establish robustness on multi-thousand-token agentic traces. Likewise, the reported ORR/IFEval/MMLU-Pro metrics do not test whether a benign long-form response is abandoned after generation begins. We will retain both as explicit limitations and remove any wording that could suggest they are resolved. Inference-time any-depth defenses are complementary to CURE-DPO, but their composition has not been evaluated here; we will clarify this positioning rather than claim an empirical combination benefit.

Overall, we will revise the paper to distinguish three levels of evidence more clearly: improvements on the constructed training-grid setting, transfer to unseen and checkpoint-generated states, and the important deployment regimes that remain untested.
